# Living user rules

Update this file when the user gives a durable manuscript-writing or notation rule that should apply to later papers.

The complete source inventory, including non-manuscript writing preferences and explicit unknown boundaries, is retained in `user-writing-requirements-and-preferences.md`. This file operationalizes only the durable cross-manuscript subset.

## Current rules

- Communicate with the user in Chinese by default unless another language is requested. Lead with the central technical judgment, then give the reasoning, assumptions, and actionable details. Distinguish facts, proved conclusions, inferences, proposals, and unresolved hypotheses.
- Keep one clear, retellable technical main line. Every added model component, loss, theorem, subsection, or experiment must serve that line; remove, merge, or downgrade branches that add complexity without a necessary mechanism, result, or validation role.
- Do not turn a local question into a different paper, package a definitional fact as a contribution, or retain an attractive route that requires unavailable information or disproportionate computation. Preserve an infeasible but potentially useful route as an explicitly scoped alternative rather than blending its assumptions into the selected method.
- Use formal English suitable for IEEE Transactions, with connected technical paragraphs and explicit causal transitions. Avoid stacking declarative statements without sentence variety; employ active and passive voice, subordinate clauses, participial phrases, prepositional phrases, adverbial modifiers, and appropriate adjectives and adverbs to create natural academic flow.
- Use participial phrases and prepositional phrases to create natural flow and avoid excessive simple sentences. Common academic patterns include:
  - Purpose/basis: "Based on [method], ", "Using [technique], ", "By [action], "
    Example: "By choosing appropriate inductive bias for the weight function, the model provides quality-relevant information"
  - Cause/reason: "Owing to [factor], ", "Due to [constraint], ", "Because of [limitation], "
    Example: "Owing to their explicit projection objectives, the extracted latent variables are usually meaningful"
  - Benefit: "Benefiting from [advantage], "
    Example: "Benefiting from the single-layer structure and explicit projection objectives, MMs generated by shallow learning methods are inferable, traceable, and interpretable"
  - Concession: "Although [fact], ", "Despite [limitation], "
    Example: "Although many deep learning-based imputation models have been proposed, superior imputation strategies are rare"
- Write with clear causality within and between paragraphs. Within a paragraph, state the reason (why this is needed) before the result (what was done). Between paragraphs, progress from macro to micro: present the overall system architecture and objective before decomposing into module-level structures and parameter-level details. Follow the exposition order "system definition → structure stacking → component design → detail explanation" rather than introducing variables before their context.
- Write with purpose and thematic focus. Every paragraph must serve one identifiable role: introduce or clarify a construction, support a contribution claim, establish a logical transition, or advance the argument. Remove content that merely pads word count or repeats information without adding analytical depth.
- Prefer connected academic paragraphs over code-like prose or unexplained abbreviations. Define each abbreviation at first use and keep it stable throughout the manuscript.
- For Chinese manuscripts, use concise terminology and sentence structure natural to control, automation, process-systems, and fuzzy-systems journals rather than translating long English sentences mechanically. When a project requires bilingual output, keep terminology, notation, claims, and section logic aligned across versions.
- For Chinese manuscripts, use Chinese names for theorem, assumption, lemma, proposition, corollary, remark, and proof environments unless the target template dictates otherwise. The result must read as a submission-shaped scientific paper, not a translated dialogue.
- For English manuscripts, rewrite the argument according to IEEE Transactions logic rather than translating Chinese sentence by sentence. Avoid Chinglish, repeated shortcuts such as “Obviously” and “It is easy to see,” unsupported superiority claims such as “significantly outperforms,” and absolute priority claims such as “the first.”
- Use established technical terms rather than metaphorical labels such as “pipeline,” “protection layer,” “magic,” “Hogwarts-style,” “signature,” “certificate,” or improvised protection language when the intended object is a reference trajectory, residual, condition, bound, or diagnostic channel. In Chinese observer and filtering prose, translate the estimator term *innovation* as “新息” rather than the novelty-related “创新”.
- When a term such as “nominal/标称” can refer to several objects or regimes, name the precise object, use a less ambiguous standard alternative where possible, or define the term at first use.
- Place no displayed equation before Section II; keep the title, abstract, keywords, and Introduction formula-free except for indispensable inline symbols.
- Use the compact main-section sequence Introduction; Preliminaries and Problem Formulation; Proposed Method; an optional section for a genuinely second task; Experiments; Conclusion. Within the method, present theoretical modeling/analysis before data-driven forward modeling and loss design, and finish with the end-to-end workflow; put long proofs in appendices.
- Recheck the actual chapter and subsection arrangement against that compact sequence in every writing loop. Record and justify any deviation by the target journal, scientific dependency, or a genuinely independent second task; drafting history or a desire for more headings is not a justification.
- Structure the Introduction as background and problem, modeling-method categories, problem-focused literature and limitations, the core question, contributions, and finally section organization plus notation conventions.
- Keep Section II as background/problem formulation; place paper-specific innovations in Section III or the designated method section. End Section II with a mathematically stated `Problem Formulation` or `Problem Description`, use one numbered item for each central subproblem, and normally retain two or three items. Do not substitute a long prose checklist such as `Monitoring Objectives`. Section II should contain only standard, established background definitions (e.g., basic T-S fuzzy system definitions, standard Koopman operator concepts) and must not include paper-specific constructions, novel combinations, attention mechanisms, or method innovations—those belong in Section III.
- Define every symbol at first use, or define a large coherent symbol family in the Notation paragraph at the end of the Introduction.
- Choose each new symbol by this order: the target journal's or research field's established notation, a standard mathematical convention, a semantically meaningful English initial or mnemonic, and only then another unclaimed symbol with an explicit project-specific rationale. Record that naming basis in the Markdown notation ledger. A mnemonic must not override a stronger field convention, create a misleading association, or collide with an existing meaning, object type, or semantic family.
- When naming variables, prioritize semantic mnemonics that reflect the quantity's physical or mathematical role, following field conventions:
  - Use superscripts for status/mode indicators: x^n for normal, x^f for faulty
  - Use subscripts for component/time indices: h^(1), h^(2), ... for layer indices
  - Avoid overloading common letters:
    * Reserve D for discriminator, decoder, or domain (not dimension)
    * Reserve G for generator (in GAN contexts)
    * Reserve L for loss or layer count (avoid for sequence length)
    * Reserve θ for trainable parameters
  - For compound models, use consistent prefix/superscript families:
    * Example: W^(1), b^(1) for encoder layer 1; W^o, b^o for output layer
    * Example: ℒ^+(·) for positive feedback loss, ℒ^-(·) for negative feedback loss
  - Document the naming convention explicitly when introducing a multi-layer or multi-stage system
- Define every set, index range, dimension, matrix partition, initial condition, and data source at first use. Do not introduce a symbol merely because its shape appears inferable from context.
- Keep one semantic family per base character across lowercase/uppercase and font variants, and keep one object type per visual format. A change from italic to bold, calligraphic, script, or blackboard-bold does not by itself justify an unrelated meaning. Reserve `f`/`F` for fault or faulty quantities, `n` for normal/nominal quantities rather than dimensions, `k` for discrete sample indices, and `u` for control inputs. Use a dedicated dimension prefix such as `m`, keep disturbance/noise symbols disjoint from weight/whitening symbols such as `w`/`W`/`\omega`, and use boldface for vectors/matrices, calligraphic capitals for mappings, blackboard bold for sets/spaces, and one dedicated operator font. Descriptive roman-text subscripts and universally standard operators may be retained when they cannot be mistaken for independent variables.
- Avoid multiple visually similar Greek letters for the same concept; use one observable/lifting symbol family and distinguish scalar/vector members by indices and formatting.
- Use calligraphic capitals for nonlinear mappings and blackboard-bold notation only for number fields, domains, sets, and spaces. Use ordinary bold vectors or matrices—not blackboard-bold letters—for stacked histories, information variables, states, and data arrays.
- Use standard LaTeX notation consistently for scalars, vectors, matrices, block matrices, transposes, norms, expectations, probabilities, partial derivatives, and operators. Do not compose mathematical objects from ordinary prose characters or use the same unqualified typography for a scalar and a vector.
- Avoid code-style stacking notation such as `\operatorname{col}`. Display vectors and block matrices explicitly with brackets and transposes so their orientation is mathematically unambiguous.
- Within the reserved fault family, use one visually consistent form such as `f`/`\mathfrak f`; keep actuator and sensor fault channels distinct and combine them only in a clearly defined generalized fault vector.
- Reserve `H` for the neural/history-network side; do not reuse it for a physical output mapping.
- Do not assign the `H` family simultaneously to a history object and a Hilbert space. Register one of them with a different unclaimed base character before drafting; do not rely on font variation alone to distinguish the meanings.
- When an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is used, denote that space and its infinite-dimensional character explicitly. The legacy example `\mathbb H^{\infty}` is acceptable only when the `H` base family is not already reserved for a history or neural object; otherwise choose a registered, unclaimed space character.
- Avoid symbol collisions between Koopman operators/matrices and attention keys; use disjoint characters or fonts.
- Combine related multi-line equations into one numbered formula using `equation` plus `aligned`/`split`.
- Keep two or three principal contribution items. Use the same count and order as the central subproblems in Section II, and map every item to one explicit construction or result in the body. Do not split construction, theory, and validation into separate contribution bullets when they address the same subproblem.
- Preserve the legacy requirement for three to five concrete, verifiable contribution claims when a target venue or a technically dense paper needs construction, analysis, and validation stated separately. These are subordinate claims mapped under the two or three principal contribution themes, not three to five unrelated main lines; the Introduction must make that hierarchy explicit.
- Keep the abstract concise and result-led. Unless the target journal imposes a different limit, use 150–220 English words and preserve the same problem and result order used by the problem statement and contribution list.
- Every central theorem must support an identified contribution, but not every contribution requires a theorem; constructions, algorithms, complexity results, and experiments may provide the appropriate support.
- Keep the abstract, problem formulation, Introduction contribution themes, body outputs, and Conclusion synchronized. Background, a general experimental observation, or an implementation detail is not a standalone contribution, and the Conclusion must not enlarge a result beyond what the abstract, proof, and evidence support.
- Before a theorem, explain which question it resolves and why the result is needed. After it, state its design meaning, implementation meaning, scope, or limitation rather than leaving the formal statement disconnected from the method.
- Treat sentence-to-sentence logic as a primary quality condition. Every sentence must have a clear role, referent, and dependency on the preceding argument; remove unexplained topic jumps, ambiguous pronouns, repeated claims, and transitions that merely place sentences next to one another.
- Build the narrative through explicit causality: establish the problem or limitation, explain why it requires the proposed construction, derive how the construction acts, state what follows, and show how the evidence tests that consequence. Do not present chronology, correlation, or module order as a causal explanation.
- Audit formula rigor at every subsection: definitions and assumptions must precede use; indices, dimensions, operators, evaluation points, equality or inequality conditions, intermediate steps, and conclusion scope must be complete and mutually consistent.
- Describe every model as a closed information and computation system. Identify its inputs, outputs, states or latent variables, known and unavailable quantities, fixed and learned mappings, parameters, assumptions, update order, and interface with the next component.
- Describe training, validation, testing, and online deployment as separate, reproducible flows. State the data source and split, permitted inputs and labels, losses and constraints, optimization and model-selection rule, test-only operations, metrics, initialization, and offline/online boundary; explicitly prevent information leakage.
- Use standard field-specific academic terminology rather than literal or improvised phrases such as `healthy transition`; choose `nominal`, `fault-free`, or `normal-operation` according to the precise object.
- Remove drafting commentary, author-process notes, self-explanatory asides, and previews of where paper-specific material will appear. State the necessary definition or argument directly; retain only conventional roadmaps and technically necessary cross-references.
- Preserve useful technical content, formulas, attention mechanisms, fuzzy memberships, model structures, theoretical results, and reference organization from an existing manuscript unless deletion is explicitly authorized. Reorganize valid content around the corrected main line instead of producing a layout-only rewrite.
- When revising an existing manuscript, report which material was preserved, strengthened, merged, or removed and why. Delete only material that is invalid, conflicts with the selected main line, or is materially redundant.
- When a complete manuscript is requested, deliver a submission-shaped paper rather than research notes or a list of suggestions. Apart from genuinely unavailable evidence, retain the abstract, Introduction, problem formulation, method, theory, workflow or algorithm, complexity, experiments or experiment design, and Conclusion.
- Record only cross-manuscript writing and reasoning rules in this file; do not turn one paper's temporary notation, equation numbering, or technical design choice into a global rule.

## Formal-claim taxonomy (authoritative)

Use this as the only taxonomy for theorem-like environments. Do not restate a parallel taxonomy elsewhere in the skill or manuscript instructions.

- Reserve **Theorem** for a central, genuinely original, broadly applicable result of the paper. State complete assumptions and an exact conclusion, and provide a rigorous proof; move a long proof to an appendix. A routine consequence, narrowly scoped result, or informally justified claim is not a theorem.
- Present an imported supporting result from prior work as a **Lemma**, with its assumptions and an exact citation, and make its non-original status explicit. Do not relabel a cited result as the paper's own theorem. If the manuscript materially adapts or extends it, identify and prove the new part separately.
- Use **Corollary (推论)** for a result derived directly from a cited lemma or a theorem established in the manuscript. Name the parent result and provide an explicit proof, even when the proof is brief.
- Use **Proposition (命题)** for a verifiable but narrower, less general, or more context-dependent mathematical result. State its assumptions and provide a proof. Limited generality permits the proposition label; it does not permit an unsupported claim.
- Use **Remark (备注)** only for interpretation, scope, limitations, intuition, or implementation guidance. A remark must not hide a new mathematical guarantee; relabel such a guarantee as a proposition or corollary and prove it.
- Minimize theorem count. Normally retain only one or two central theorems; use more only when every theorem is independently central, genuinely original, and rigorously proved. Merge closely related statements and downgrade routine or local claims according to this taxonomy.
- Before delivery, inventory every theorem-like environment, verify its label, originality, assumptions, proof or citation, and dependency on prior results, and remove duplicate or contradictory classification rules.

## Update protocol

When a later user message adds or changes a rule, append or revise the smallest relevant authoritative passage only if it generalizes beyond the current manuscript. For each rule family, retain one noncontradictory source of truth: merge overlaps and delete or rewrite conflicts instead of appending duplicates. Keep paper-specific conclusions in the active task notes or manuscript, not here.
